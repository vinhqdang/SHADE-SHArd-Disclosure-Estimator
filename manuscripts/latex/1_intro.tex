\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The rapid proliferation of Large Language Models (LLMs) has fundamentally altered the landscape of natural language processing and enterprise knowledge management. While early monolithic models relied entirely on their parametric memory—knowledge implicitly encoded within billions of synaptic weights during pre-training—they inherently suffered from static knowledge cutoff and catastrophic hallucination rates. These limitations have been largely circumvented by the advent of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \cite{lewis2020rag}. RAG architectures bifurcate the intelligence of the system: preserving the LLM as a stateless reasoning engine while delegating factual recall to a dynamic, non-parametric external memory, typically instantiated as a dense vector database.

This architectural shift has unlocked unprecedented utility, enabling organizations to deploy specialized AI agents capable of reasoning over proprietary, highly sensitive corpora, ranging from legal case files and financial disclosures to electronic health records (EHRs). However, the centralization of this sensitive non-parametric memory introduces severe security and privacy vulnerabilities. Standard RAG implementations process and store entire corporate knowledge bases as plaintext or easily inverted dense embeddings \cite{morris2023vec2text} within a single monolithic vector database. Consequently, the retrieval database has become the ultimate high-value target for both external exfiltration and internal privilege escalation attacks. If an adversary gains access to this centralized repository—or successfully mounts an extraction attack via the local LLM interface \cite{carlini2021extract, zeng2024ragprivacy}—the entire intellectual property of the enterprise is compromised.

To mitigate these catastrophic risks, security-conscious organizations are increasingly abandoning monolithic centralized databases in favor of distributed, multi-tenant architectures \cite{wang2024mrag}. In these distributed deployments, the underlying knowledge base is explicitly partitioned into multiple physically and logically isolated storage units, or \textit{shards}. When a user issues a query, the RAG orchestration layer routes the query only to the relevant shards, retrieving isolated context fragments and aggregating them securely. The foundational security premise of semantic sharding is isolation: an adversary who successfully compromises a single shard—or an adversary monitoring the traffic to a specific local LLM instance—should only observe fragmented, incoherent data, rendering the underlying sensitive topic impossible to reconstruct.

However, the design of the partitioning algorithm dictates the success or failure of this security premise. The standard industry approach to partitioning relies on unsupervised semantic clustering, primarily K-Means or advanced community detection algorithms like Leiden modularity \cite{ng2001spectral}, which aim to maximize the intra-shard semantic coherence. While this effectively optimizes retrieval utility and minimizes cross-shard latency, our research reveals a critical vulnerability: these algorithms inadvertently co-locate all documents pertaining to a specific sensitive topic onto the exact same shard. If a patient's entire medical history is mapped to Shard A to optimize retrieval speed, the compromise of Shard A guarantees the total, unmitigated disclosure of that patient's clinical profile.

Conversely, aggressive privacy-preserving frameworks such as VertiSplitRAG \cite{yang2025splitrag} and DPVoteRAG explicitly scramble the semantic layout to enforce strict data isolation and differential privacy guarantees. While these frameworks successfully thwart reconstruction attacks, they inherently destroy the semantic proximity required by dense retrievers (e.g., DPR \cite{karpukhin2020dpr}), leading to catastrophic degradations in RAG utility and downstream generation accuracy. This persistent, unquantified tension between utility and privacy necessitates a formal, mathematically rigorous framework for semantic partitioning.

In this paper, we bridge this critical gap. We formalize the tension between RAG utility and data security, proving that optimizing one fundamentally degrades the other—establishing the No-Free-Lunch theorem of semantic sharding. To navigate this tradeoff, we introduce the SHArd Disclosure Estimator (SHADE), a novel, mathematically rigorous metric that quantifies the risk of an adversary reconstructing a contiguous semantic topic from any single compromised shard. Building upon this metric, we propose CoShard, a graph-based partitioning algorithm that explicitly integrates the SHADE penalty into its modularity maximization objective. By formally penalizing the co-location of highly related sensitive documents, CoShard forces the distribution of conceptual topics across multiple isolated shards, intentionally starving potential adversaries of the context required for semantic reconstruction.

The core contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Formalization of the Semantic Reconstruction Threat Model:} We formalize a novel threat model specific to distributed RAG architectures, detailing how an adversary observing a single compromised shard can leverage state-of-the-art embedding inversion attacks \cite{li2023geia} to reconstruct highly sensitive corporate topics.
    \item \textbf{The SHADE Metric:} We introduce the SHArd Disclosure Estimator (SHADE), a quantifiable, model-agnostic metric that calculates the mathematical risk of topic disclosure based on the graph topology of the partitioned vector space.
    \item \textbf{The CoShard Algorithm:} We propose CoShard, a highly scalable, two-phase community detection algorithm that natively incorporates the SHADE metric as an optimization constraint. CoShard allows system administrators to seamlessly tune a hyperparameter $\lambda$ to navigate the precise optimal boundary between retrieval utility and cryptographic privacy.
    \item \textbf{Information-Theoretic Privacy Guarantees:} We provide rigorous mathematical proofs leveraging the Data Processing Inequality and conditional entropy to guarantee that, under CoShard partitioning, the mutual information available to an adversary falls strictly below the critical ambiguity threshold required for semantic reconstruction.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Empirical Evaluation:} We conduct exhaustive experiments over two critical enterprise benchmarks, MedQA (medical) and LegalBench (legal). Our results empirically confirm the No-Free-Lunch theorem of RAG partitioning and demonstrate that CoShard strictly dominates standard baselines (K-Means, GraphRAG) and state-of-the-art privacy frameworks (VertiSplitRAG) by successfully tracing the Pareto optimal frontier.
\end{itemize}
